Foundations

A bakery with two ovens: look at the data, ask one question, meet outliers, many tests, exact counts, and the same bytes everywhere.

1 · Before anything else, look

start heredescribe

“I bought a second oven. Is it any good?” — Marta, who bakes for a street of cafes

Marta kept a note of every batch for two months: which oven, how many minutes, how high it rose, how the crust scored, whether a loaf came out defective. Eighty batches, forty per oven. Before she asks anything, she looks at the one number she cares about most, the baking time.

2 · Is the new oven faster?

one questiontest t2ci diff

“Eyeballing it, B is quicker. Show me it is not my imagination.”

The textbook answer is Welch’s t test: compare the two means, allow the spreads to differ. The tool prints the statistic, the degrees of freedom, the p-value, an effect size and the interval of the difference. The same interval is available alone with ci diff, byte for byte.

a real run
$ stats test t2 --cols bake_min --group oven bakery.csv
{
  "levels": {"a": "A", "b": "B"},
  "tests": [
    {
      "statistic": 1.8222469060619957,
      "df": 73.1757032479013,
      "p_value": 0.0725033707662692,
      "effect_size": {"name": "cohen_d", "value": 0.4074667953743296},
      "interval": {
        "of": "difference of means",
        "level": 0.95,
        "estimate": 1.9775000000000005,
        "low": -0.18521043371277957,
        "high": 4.140210433712781,
        "rounding": "double"
      },
      "n": {"a": 40, "b": 40}
    }
  ]
}
Reading it. A is 1.98 minutes slower on average, with a 95% interval from -0.185 to 4.14 and p = 0.0725. The interval reaches across zero, so at the 5% level this does not show a difference. (ci diff prints the same interval: -0.185 – 4.14.) Marta is not convinced, and she is not wrong to be careful — but the pictures in story 1 suggested something. What is it that a mean cannot see?

3 · Three loaves ran long

when the data misbehavetest mannwhitneyresample perm

“Three batches in oven B took over fifty minutes. The timer stuck, I think. I did not throw them away.”

Every batch as a dot shows what the means hid: oven B’s typical batch is faster than A’s, and three batches — ringed — are far to the right. A mean is pulled by such points; a test that compares ranks is not. The Mann–Whitney test asks whether a batch from one oven tends to beat a batch from the other. The permutation test below asks the question of the means directly, by shuffling which batch went to which oven.

a real run
$ stats test mannwhitney --cols bake_min --group oven bakery.csv
{
  "tests": [
    {
      "statistic": 1107.0,
      "p_value": 0.0031799854079099642,
      "p_method": "normal",
      "effect_size": {"name": "rank_biserial", "value": 0.38375},
      "n": {"a": 40, "b": 40}
    }
  ]
}
a real run
$ stats resample perm --cols bake_min --group oven --seed 7 --permutations 9999 \
    bakery.csv
{
  "p_method": "monte-carlo",
  "seed": 7,
  "tests": [
    {
      "statistic": 1.9775000000000005,
      "p": 0.0703,
      "extreme": 702,
      "permutations": 9999,
      "mc_error": 0.0025566471489950364
    }
  ]
}
Reading it. The tests that use the mean (both t tests, and the permutation test of the mean difference) all say “not shown”, because three long batches inflate B’s mean and its spread. The rank test, which looks at the bulk of the batches, says p = 0.00318. Neither is “the right one”: they answer different questions, and the answer says which one it answered. The permutation test's count is exact (702 of 9999 shuffles were as extreme; the Monte Carlo error is printed, 0.0026), and every one of those numbers can be reproduced by an independent program from the seed. What Marta now knows: the typical batch of B is faster; the average is dragged by three exceptions she can investigate.

4 · Six questions at once

many tests, one decisiontest t2--correct holm

“While I am at it: height, crust, weight, moisture, salt. Did the new oven change any of them?”

Ask six questions at the 5% level and the chance that at least one comes out “significant” by luck alone is about one in four. The honest way to run a family of tests is to say so up front. --correct holm declares the six tests a family and prints each p-value both as computed and after Holm’s step-down correction; the tool refuses a family larger than --max-tests, so a fishing trip has to be declared.

a real run
$ stats test t2 --cols bake_min,height_cm,crust,weight_g,moisture_pct,salt_pct --group \
    oven --correct holm bakery.csv
{
  "adjustment": "holm",
  "family": {
    "size": 6,
    "correction": "holm",
    "p_adjusted": [
      0.2900134830650768,
      0.0010895185787315627,
      1.0,
      1.0,
      0.0005220022161199081,
      1.0
    ]
  },
  "tests": [
    {"column": "bake_min", "p_value": 0.0725033707662692},
    {"column": "height_cm", "p_value": 0.00021790371574631254},
    {"column": "crust", "p_value": 0.5766632775939438},
    {"column": "weight_g", "p_value": 0.5962294964274081},
    {"column": "moisture_pct", "p_value": 8.700036935331803e-05},
    {"column": "salt_pct", "p_value": 0.6300676855585874}
  ]
}
Reading it. Height and moisture still stand out after the correction; the crust, the weight and the salt do not, and neither does the baking-time mean (the story 3 problem again). The hollow and filled circles are the same test before and after the family is accounted for. Marta can now say which of her improvements are real and which could be six coin flips.

5 · Is it luck? Count it

exact answers to small questionsresample perm --permutations alltest fisher

“In the first week I only had four batches from each oven. Could four and four have told me anything?”

With eight batches, “by luck” can be counted. Hand any four of the eight batches to oven A and the other four to oven B: there are 70 ways. For each, compute the difference of the means; the p-value is the fraction of ways at least as far from zero as the real one. --permutations all does exactly that, and the comparison is made on exact integers, with no tolerance. The first four batches of each oven were 41.2, 38.9, 43.2, 40.8 minutes in A and 33.9, 40.7, 43.5, 42.9 in B: not obviously different, and the count will say how different “not obviously” is.

a real run
$ stats resample perm --cols bake_min --group oven --permutations all pilot.csv
{
  "p_method": "exact",
  "tests": [
    {
      "statistic": 0.7750000000000004,
      "p": 0.8,
      "extreme": 56,
      "permutations": 70,
      "n": {"a": 4, "b": 4, "missing": 0}
    }
  ]
}

Why not do the same on all eighty batches? Because there are about 1023 ways to split them, and the tool says so instead of trying:

a real run
$ stats resample perm --cols bake_min --group oven --permutations all bakery.csv
exit 8
{
  "code": "PRECONDITION_FAILED",
  "rule": "limit.exact-too-large",
  "message": "more arrangements of the labels than the exact test enumerates",
  "hint": "use --permutations N for the Monte Carlo test",
  "detail": {"limit": 1048576, "values": 80}
}
Reading it. 56 of 70 arrangements are as extreme as the real one (the red squares), so p = 0.8 — the exact permutation p-value, not an approximation. Four against four cannot show a difference this small, and the count says so in the plainest way there is. There is no seed in this answer because nothing was drawn.

The same idea decides the yes/no question. Over all eighty batches, 12 of 40 loaves from oven A had a defect and 1 of 40 from B. Fisher’s exact test enumerates every table with the same margins:

a real run
$ stats test fisher --cols defect --vs oven bakery.csv
{
  "tests": [
    {
      "p_value": 0.0014946381722911106,
      "p_method": "exact, probabilities not larger than the observed",
      "effect_size": {"name": "odds_ratio", "value": 0.05982905982905983},
      "assumptions": {
        "levels": {"column": ["n", "y"], "vs": ["A", "B"]},
        "table": [28, 39, 12, 1]
      }
    }
  ]
}
Reading it. p = 0.00149, from enumerating the tables, not from a formula that is only good for large counts: oven B has far fewer defective loaves, and this is the question on which the evidence is clearest. The odds ratio is printed too.

“My accountant will run this on her laptop, and the cafe’s analyst on a server. Will they get my numbers?”

The sums are exact, so the order in which rows are added cannot change a bit; the exact permutation test depends only on which values are in each group. Here is the proof you can run yourself: the same questions on the same data with the rows in three orders, and the SHA-256 of everything the tool printed.

Reading it. All three digests of each question are identical. Medicine and biology

Small samples, exact answers, a randomized trial with three endpoints, and what zero side effects can and cannot say.

7 · Six mice against six

biology: small samples, exact answerstest t2test mannwhitneyresample perm --permutations all

“Six wild-type and six knockout mice, one liver sample each. The knockout looks higher. One animal is very high. Is the gene doing something?” — a postdoc, late on a Friday

Twelve animals is a small sample, and an assumption that the data are bell-shaped cannot be checked on twelve points. Three questions, from the most assumption-laden to the least: Welch’s t test (needs the means to be roughly normal), the Mann–Whitney test (ranks only; with no ties and so few animals the tool computes the exact p-value, p_method: exact, not the normal approximation), and a permutation test of the means over all 924 ways to hand six of the twelve animals to the knockout group.

a real run
$ stats test mannwhitney --cols expression --group genotype bio.csv
{
  "tests": [
    {
      "statistic": 32.0,
      "p_value": 0.025974025974025976,
      "p_method": "exact",
      "effect_size": {"name": "rank_biserial", "value": 0.7777777777777778},
      "n": {"a": 6, "b": 6}
    }
  ]
}
a real run
$ stats resample perm --cols expression --group genotype --permutations all bio.csv
{
  "p_method": "exact",
  "tests": [
    {
      "statistic": 1.028333333333333,
      "p": 0.023809523809523808,
      "extreme": 22,
      "permutations": 924,
      "n": {"a": 6, "b": 6, "missing": 0}
    }
  ]
}
Reading it. Welch’s p is 0.0705: the one very high knockout animal inflates the knockout’s variance, and the test is cautious. The exact rank test gives p = 0.026 and the exact permutation test p = 0.0238 (22 of 924 arrangements as extreme). None of these is a Monte Carlo estimate: there is no seed because nothing was drawn. In a small experiment the honest sentence is the one with the method in it: by an exact permutation test, p = 0.0238.

8 · Did the drug lower the blood pressure?

medicine: a randomized trial, three endpointstest t2ci diff--correct holm

“Eight weeks, 120 patients, half on the drug and half on a placebo, assigned by a coin. The primary question is the systolic pressure. We also looked at the diastolic pressure and the heart rate.” — the trial statistician, before unblinding the report

One question was written down before the trial: does the drug change the systolic pressure more than the placebo does? One test answers it, and an interval says by how much.

a real run
$ stats test t2 --cols sbp_change --group arm trial.csv
{
  "levels": {"a": "drug", "b": "placebo"},
  "tests": [
    {
      "statistic": -3.2318462005889708,
      "df": 117.75621854173842,
      "p_value": 0.0015952704334033319,
      "effect_size": {"name": "cohen_d", "value": -0.5900516888166484},
      "n": {"a": 60, "b": 60}
    }
  ]
}
a real run
$ stats ci diff --cols sbp_change --group arm trial.csv
{
  "intervals": [
    {
      "estimate": -5.8116666666666665,
      "interval": {"low": -9.372767142085737, "high": -2.250566191247597}
    }
  ]
}
Reading it. The drug lowers the systolic pressure by 5.8 mmHg more than the placebo (95% interval 2.3 to 9.4; Welch’s t test p = 0.0016). That is the pre-specified answer, and it does not need a correction: it is one question.

The two other measurements are another matter. Three questions asked of the same patients, at the 5% level each, and the chance that at least one comes out significant by luck alone is about one in seven. Holm’s correction keeps that chance at 5% for the family: the smallest p-value is multiplied by the number of tests, the next by one fewer, and so on.

a real run
$ stats test t2 --cols sbp_change,dbp_change,hr_change --group arm --correct holm \
    trial.csv
{
  "tests": [
    {"column": "sbp_change", "p_value": 0.0015952704334033319},
    {"column": "dbp_change", "p_value": 0.0439353947110954},
    {"column": "hr_change", "p_value": 0.856703410390581}
  ],
  "family": {
    "size": 3,
    "correction": "holm",
    "p_adjusted": [0.004785811300209996, 0.0878707894221908, 0.856703410390581]
  }
}
Reading it. The systolic result stands after the correction (p = 0.0048). The diastolic pressure has p = 0.044 on its own, which would pass 0.05, and 0.088 after Holm, which does not: with three endpoints looked at, that result is what a family of tests would produce by chance one time in several, and the report should call it suggestive, not shown. The heart rate shows nothing (p = 0.86). Which questions were pre-specified, and how many were asked, is part of the result.

9 · No serious events: how safe is that?

medicine: side effects and the zero that proves littletest fisherci prop --method clopper-pearson

“Nausea was more common on the drug. And nobody had a serious adverse event, so the drug is safe.” — a sentence from a first draft of the safety section

Side effects are counts, often small ones, and the arithmetic of small counts is exactly what Fisher’s exact test is for: it lists every table with the same margins and adds up those at least as unlikely as the observed one.

a real run
$ stats test fisher --cols nausea --vs arm events.csv
{
  "tests": [
    {
      "p_value": 0.01944841662107582,
      "p_method": "exact, probabilities not larger than the observed",
      "effect_size": {"name": "odds_ratio", "value": 0.23469387755102042},
      "interval": null
    }
  ]
}
a real run
$ stats test fisher --cols dizziness --vs arm events.csv
{
  "tests": [
    {
      "p_value": 1.0,
      "p_method": "exact, probabilities not larger than the observed"
    }
  ]
}
Reading it. Nausea: 14 of 60 on the drug against 4 of 60 on the placebo, Fisher p = 0.0194. Dizziness: 1 against 2, p = 1.000, no difference that 120 patients can show. The nausea result is a real signal to put in the label; the dizziness one is not a signal in either direction.

Then the sentence from the draft. Zero events in 120 patients. The exact (Clopper–Pearson) interval for a proportion says what rates are still compatible with seeing none:

a real run
$ stats ci prop --cols serious --success y --method clopper-pearson events.csv
{
  "intervals": [
    {
      "estimate": 0.0,
      "interval": {"method": "clopper-pearson", "low": 0.0, "high": 0.03027297257742006},
      "n": {"n": 120, "successes": 0, "missing": 0}
    }
  ]
}
Reading it. No serious event in 120 patients is compatible with a true rate of up to 3.0%, about three in a hundred (the “rule of three”: three divided by the number of patients). A serious event that happens to one patient in a hundred could easily go unseen in a trial this size. The honest sentence is “no serious adverse event was observed; the data cannot exclude a rate up to 3.0%”, and a drug taken by millions needs a monitoring plan for what 120 patients cannot see.

Chemistry and physics

10 · Two methods, one sample set

chemistry: correlation is not agreementtest pairedci corr

“The UV method is ten times cheaper and it correlates almost perfectly with the HPLC. Can we switch?” — the lab manager

Twelve caffeine samples were measured by both methods. A correlation coefficient asks whether the two move together; it says nothing about whether they give the same number. The tool answers both questions on the same file: ci corr with a Fisher-z interval for the correlation, and a paired test of the differences, whose mean is the bias between the methods.

a real run
$ stats ci corr --cols hplc,uv chem.csv
{
  "intervals": [
    {
      "x": "hplc",
      "y": "uv",
      "n": 12,
      "estimate": 0.9993345182683051,
      "interval": {
        "of": "correlation",
        "method": "fisher-z",
        "level": 0.95,
        "estimate": 0.9993345182683051,
        "low": 0.9975440770330842,
        "high": 0.9998197920937946,
        "rounding": "double"
      }
    }
  ]
}
a real run
$ stats test paired --pairs hplc:uv chem.csv
{
  "tests": [
    {
      "pair": ["hplc", "uv"],
      "statistic": -2.568943422391961,
      "df": 11,
      "p_value": 0.026099475525154844,
      "interval": {
        "of": "difference of means",
        "level": 0.95,
        "estimate": -0.7108333333333334,
        "low": -1.3198516574926078,
        "high": -0.10181500917405917,
        "rounding": "double"
      },
      "n": {"n": 12, "missing": 0}
    }

}
Reading it. The correlation is 0.99933, with a 95% interval 0.99754 to 0.99982: the methods move together almost perfectly. The paired differences have a mean of -0.711 mg with a 95% interval from -1.32 to -0.102, which excludes zero (p = 0.0261): the UV method reads systematically higher, here by 1.6 per cent of the mean amount. A calibration against the HPLC would fix a bias like this; a correlation of 0.999 could never have shown it. The figure on the right is the same finding as a picture.

11 · Same source, two detectors

physics: shape, and digits that canceltest t2test ks --exact yesdescribe

“Both detectors see the 662 keV line of the same caesium source. Their average energies agree. Why does the second one look broader?” — a detector physicist

Thirty counts on each detector. The second detector’s gain drifted during the run, so its line is split into two close peaks. A comparison of means cannot see that; the two-sample Kolmogorov–Smirnov test compares the whole distributions, and with 60 values and no ties the tool computes its p-value exactly (--exact yes refuses if it cannot).

a real run
$ stats test t2 --cols energy_keV --group detector detectors.csv
{
  "tests": [
    {
      "statistic": -0.24744542916521842,
      "df": 51.937980184489305,
      "p_value": 0.8055391129701185,
      "interval": {
        "of": "difference of means",
        "level": 0.95,
        "estimate": -1.2843333333333362,
        "low": -11.69986876225348,
        "high": 9.131202095586808,
        "rounding": "double"
      },
      "n": {"a": 30, "b": 30}
    }
  ]
}
a real run
$ stats test ks --cols energy_keV --group detector --exact yes detectors.csv
{
  "tests": [
    {
      "statistic": 0.36666666666666664,
      "p_value": 0.03458008496333449,
      "p_method": "exact",
      "n": {"a": 30, "b": 30}
    }
  ]
}
Reading it. The t test sees nothing (p = 0.806): the two means differ by 1.3 keV out of 662. The largest vertical gap between the two distribution functions is D = 0.3667, and the exact p-value is 0.0346. The drawn gap is the printed statistic.

A second habit of the field: timestamps. Twenty arrivals of the same repeated event, recorded as seconds since 1970 — a number near 1.7×109 carrying microseconds of jitter. What is the spread? The textbook one-pass formula, (Σx²/n − mean²) n/(n−1), is what many spreadsheets and quick scripts compute:

a real run
$ stats describe --cols t_s clock.csv
{
  "columns": [
    {
      "count": 20,
      "mean": 1700000000.5000012,
      "variance": 8.935037505197168e-12,
      "sd": 2.989153309082217e-06
    }
  ]
}
Reading it. The tool’s variance is 8.93504e-12 s², which is the exact variance of the twenty recorded numbers rounded once (checked against exact fractions when this page is generated). The one-pass formula in doubles, run on the same twenty numbers, answers 538.947: the two terms it subtracts are about 2.9×1018 and agree to the last digit that a double holds, so what is left is rounding noise, here about 1014 times too large. The sums in stats are exact, so the offset costs nothing.

Social science

Ordinal answers and honest intervals.

12 · What does the town think?

social science: ordinal answers and honest intervalsci proptest kruskaltest chisq

“A hundred and fifty residents answered. Does the town support the new bus line? Do the districts feel the same?” — a council analyst

Survey data are counts and ordinal scales, and the usual mistakes are treating a Likert score as a measurement on a ruler and quoting a share without its uncertainty. First the share: the Wilson interval is the default, and Clopper–Pearson (the exact binomial interval) is one flag away and always the wider.

a real run
$ stats ci prop --cols support --success yes town.csv
{
  "intervals": [
    {
      "column": "support",
      "estimate": 0.58,
      "interval": {
        "of": "proportion",
        "method": "wilson",
        "level": 0.95,
        "estimate": 0.58,
        "low": 0.49998480543102514,
        "high": 0.6560199554700826,
        "rounding": "double"
      },
      "n": {"n": 150, "successes": 87, "missing": 0}
    }
  ]
}
a real run
$ stats ci prop --cols support --success yes --method clopper-pearson town.csv
{
  "intervals": [
    {
      "column": "support",
      "estimate": 0.58,
      "interval": {
        "of": "proportion",
        "method": "clopper-pearson",
        "level": 0.95,
        "estimate": 0.58,
        "low": 0.49675905312956237,
        "high": 0.6600167856652149,
        "rounding": "double"
      }
    }
  ]
}
Reading it. 0.58 of the sample support the line. Both intervals contain one half, so “a majority supports it” is not something this sample can claim; the exact interval is wider, as it must be.

Second, do the districts feel the same? Satisfaction is on a 1–5 scale: an order, not a ruler. The Kruskal–Wallis test uses only the order, and prints the number of ties and the mean rank of each district. Third, is support related to education? Counts in a table call for the chi-square test of independence; its answer carries the smallest expected count and how many are below 5, so that the reader can see the test applies.

Marketing

Incrementality: what would have happened without the campaign. A randomized holdout, the dashboard that overstates, and a regional experiment.

13 · Did the ads cause the sales?

marketing: the randomized holdoutci prop --grouptest propci diffresample perm

“The dashboard says 6.4% of the people we showed the ad to bought. Is that the ad?” — the head of growth, who has a budget meeting on Thursday

The percentage on the dashboard cannot say. Some of those buyers would have bought anyway; what the ad is worth is the extra buyers, and the only way to see people who were never shown the ad is to withhold it from some of them at random. The team did that: 8000 users, alternately assigned (the assignment is the marketer’s coin, not the user’s choice) to see the ads or to be held out. A user row is: arm, bought (0 or 1), and revenue (0 for a user who did not buy).

First the conversion rate in each arm and the difference between them, with the Newcombe interval for a difference of two proportions (the one that stays sensible when the rates are small):

a real run

Reading it. 256 of 4000 users shown the ad bought, against 200 of 4000 held out: the ad adds 1.40 points (the interval runs from 0.38 to 2.42, and the test gives p = 0.007). The ad works. And the dashboard’s 6.4% was misleading: 78 of every 100 buyers would have bought anyway, and the incremental share is the 1.40 points.

Now money. The budget meeting wants revenue, not conversions, and revenue per user is a nasty column: most users spend nothing and a few spend a lot. (The arithmetic on the printed numbers is the page’s, not the tool’s: the media cost is invented.)

14 · The dashboard that was too good to be true

marketing: why exposed-versus-not is not a liftci prop --groupdescribe --by

“We did not run a holdout for the autumn campaign, but we know who saw the ad and who did not. Why not just compare them?” — a marketer, with a spreadsheet

The autumn campaign was targeted: the platform shows the ad to users who are active, because active users click. 6000 users, 2177 of them reached. Comparing buyers among the reached with buyers among the rest is the comparison every ad dashboard makes:

a real run

A campaign worth a large budget deserves a holdout, and a small one (even 5% of the audience) costs far less than being wrong about the return.

15 · Twelve regions and a TV campaign

marketing: a geo experiment, difference in differencestest t2resample perm --permutations allci diff

“We cannot hold out individual viewers of a TV spot. We can hold out whole regions.” — a media planner

Television reaches a region, not a user, so the unit of the experiment is the region. Twelve sales regions; six were drawn at random to get the campaign for a month. Comparing the TV regions with the others after the month would mix the campaign with the fact that some regions are simply bigger. What does not depend on size is the change in each region: sales after minus sales before, in thousands of euros a week. Comparing changes between the two groups is the difference in differences, and here it is one column, change_keur, compared between two groups of six.

Six against six is a small sample, so two answers are given, the t test and the exact one: the permutation test with --permutations all lists every one of the 924 ways to choose which six regions got the campaign, and counts those at least as extreme as the one drawn.

Finance

Returns have heavy tails; a backtest that searched has to count what it tried.

16 · How bad is a bad day?

finance: returns are not normaldescribeci boot --statistic quantileci mean

“The risk report says the daily volatility is 0.9%, so a really bad day, one in a hundred, is a loss of about 2.0%. And our average day is positive.” — a risk analyst, rereading the report with some suspicion

750 trading days of a share, in percent. Before any model, the shape: the skewness and the excess kurtosis (zero for a normal distribution) say how far from a bell this is.

a real run
$ stats describe --cols ret_pct --bins 12 returns.csv
{
  "columns": [
    {
      "count": 750,
      "mean": 0.028524,
      "sd": 0.879999703443936,
      "min": -4.6,
      "max": 3.09,
      "skewness": -0.43170622907447576,
      "kurtosis": 4.08930666708214
    }
  ]
}
Reading it. The excess kurtosis is 4.1 (a normal distribution has 0), and the skewness is -0.43: more days far into the tails than a bell has, and the long tail is on the loss side (the worst day is -4.6%, 5.3 standard deviations from the mean, a day that a bell with a volatility of 0.88% would make rarer than one in a million). It happened.

The average day first, because “our average day is positive” is the sentence people repeat. The interval for the mean, then the question that matters for risk: how bad is a really bad day, the loss that is exceeded one trading day in a hundred (the 1% quantile)? The bootstrap needs no assumption about the shape, and BCa corrects its bias and skew; the seed makes it repeatable.

a real run
$ stats ci mean --cols ret_pct returns.csv
{
  "intervals": [
    {
      "estimate": 0.028524,
      "interval": {"low": -0.03455754792944781, "high": 0.0916055479294478}
    }
  ]
}
a real run
$ stats ci boot --cols ret_pct --statistic quantile --prob 0.01 --method bca --seed 7 \
    --replicates 2000 returns.csv
{
  "intervals": [
    {
      "statistic": "quantile",
      "p": 0.01,
      "estimate": -2.85806,
      "interval": {"method": "bootstrap-bca", "low": -3.753, "high": -2.32808}
    }
  ]
}
Reading it. The mean daily return is 0.029% and its interval, -0.035 to 0.092, contains zero: after 750 days the data do not show that the average day is positive. The one-in-a-hundred bad day is -2.86%, with a 95% interval from -3.75 to -2.33: one day in a hundred loses more than about 2.9%, and the whole interval lies beyond the 2.0% that the bell of the report gives. The mean is hard to know and the tail is better known (and worse than the model said): a risk report should lead with the second.

17 · Six backtests, one winner

finance: the strategy that looked good by lucktest paired--correct holm

“We tried six variants of the strategy against the index over the last 250 days. Number six beats it, p = 0.019. We should trade it.” — a portfolio manager, with a chart

Each strategy and the index have a return on each day, so the comparison is paired: the same days, the difference of the two, and a test of whether its mean is zero. The data are invented and built so that none of the six has any edge: each follows the index and adds noise.

a real run
$ stats test paired --pairs s1:index,s2:index,s3:index,s4:index,s5:index,s6:index \
    strategies.csv
{

      "p_value": 0.3259132034755653,
      "mean_difference": -0.028572

      "pair": ["s2", "index"],
      "p_value": 0.2857239863003965,
      "mean_difference": -0.029279999999999997
    },
    {
      "pair": ["s3", "index"],
      "p_value": 0.6078788115967801,
      "mean_difference": 0.014728
    },
    {
      "pair": ["s4", "index"],
      "p_value": 0.1564535187909214,
      "mean_difference": 0.041092000000000004
    },
    {
      "pair": ["s5", "index"],
      "p_value": 0.1816092081949331,

    },

      "pair": ["s6", "index"],

Reading it. One of the six, s6, passes 0.05 on its own (p = 0.019, a mean advantage of -0.066 percentage points a day). The chance that the best of six unrelated strategies passes 0.05 is about one in four, so this result is what a search over variants produces, not evidence about s6.

The remedy is to count every strategy that was tried, not only the one that was kept: Holm’s correction over the six.

a real run
$ stats test paired --pairs s1:index,s2:index,s3:index,s4:index,s5:index,s6:index \
    --correct holm strategies.csv

  "tests": [

    {"pair": ["s5", "index"], "p_value": 0.1816092081949331},

      0.8571719589011896,
      0.8571719589011896,

}
Reading it. After the correction the smallest p-value is 0.116 and nothing is left: six backtests, no evidence of an edge in any. A backtest searched over variants has to carry the number of variants tried; “we tried six and kept the best” is the sentence that makes the p-value mean what it says. (Out-of-sample days, kept aside before the search, are the other cure, and cost data.)
